About

I am a marine ecologist from coastal Ecuador. My work brings together ecology, history, fisheries and conservation sciences. I use archives, document fishers’ local ecological knowledge, and study fish biology to rebuild baselines for species and ecosystems that were changing long before scientific monitoring began.

As a postdoctoral fellow at STRI and the IAI, I assess how sensitive marine biodiversity is across the Eastern Tropical Pacific Marine Corridor (CMAR), the conservation network that links the waters of Costa Rica, Panama, Colombia and Ecuador. I also support the corridor’s member states with collaborative monitoring, stock assessments and capacity building.

Before Panama, I was a postdoctoral fellow at Trinity College Dublin, reconstructing the fish and fisheries of Tasmania since the late 19th century. My PhD, at the University of Queensland and the University of Exeter, traced the history of recreational fishing in Queensland and what it meant for vulnerable species such as giant grouper and barramundi. Along the way I have worked in Ecuador, Colombia, Panama, Germany, the United States, Australia and Ireland.

Research

Eastern Tropical Pacific

Biodiversity at risk across the Eastern Tropical Pacific Marine Corridor

Which places and lineages stand to lose the most if threatened species disappear? I combine occurrence records with IUCN Red List assessments to map taxonomic, phylogenetic and functional sensitivity for sharks and rays, bony fishes, marine mammals, seabirds and sea turtles across CMAR, so that conservation can look beyond species counts.

Current fellowship with STRI and the IAI, 2025 to 2027

Queensland and Tasmania, Australia

Reconstructing the past ocean from archives

Newspapers, fishing-club records, government reports and historical charts show what coastal ecosystems looked like before monitoring began. I’ve used them to trace the rise of recreational fishing in Southeast Queensland, the hidden decline of the giant grouper and the history of barramundi fishing, and more than a century of change in Tasmania’s fisheries and giant kelp forests.

Panama, Colombia and the Galápagos

Groupers, fishers and small-scale fisheries

Large, slow-growing groupers are among the first fish to disappear under fishing pressure, and many are too data-poor to assess. Working with fishers and fish markets, I study the age, growth and threats of the Pacific goliath grouper, and how fishing reshapes the size and sex structure of fish with complex life histories.
